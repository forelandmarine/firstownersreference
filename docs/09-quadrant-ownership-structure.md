Quadrant Group is a corporate services provider headquartered in the Isle of Man, with offices in Jersey, Malta, and Palma, and forty years in the marine sector. It designs and administers the ownership structures a first-time buyer puts in place around a yacht. The independence test set out earlier in this chapter applies to Quadrant as it applies to any firm: Quadrant provides the ownership-structure services this section describes. The material below is drawn from a written exchange with the firm's Simon Roberts, edited for length and house style. Where a position is Quadrant's judgment rather than a settled matter of law, it is attributed as one.

## The structure follows the objectives, not the other way around

The first mistake in ownership structure is treating it as an administrative detail to be settled after the yacht is found. A first-time buyer's attention is on the search, the negotiation, and the delivery, and the ownership vehicle is easy to file under paperwork for later. Roberts' position is that it is one of the earliest and most consequential decisions a buyer makes, and that the common framing of it, personal ownership against a company, is the wrong frame. The structure should be built around what the owner intends to do with the yacht, not selected from a menu and then fitted to the owner afterward.

A short set of questions determines almost everything that follows. Will the yacht be for private enjoyment only, or is charter a possibility, even if not immediately? Where will it primarily operate? Will finance be involved? Will ownership stay with one individual, or is family, succession, or more than one beneficial owner in view? How and where will the yacht be imported, and what is its VAT position? On Quadrant's account, the costly mistakes are rarely the result of choosing the wrong structure. They are the result of these questions being asked too late, once the purchase is underway and decisions have started to run on the transaction's timetable rather than the owner's. Changes that would have been straightforward before completion become difficult and expensive after it.

The second assumption worth retiring early is that the current ownership model is permanent. A yacht bought for private use may later be offered for charter. Family circumstances change, financing is introduced, ownership passes to the next generation. A structure that works on the day of purchase may not work as well five years on, which is the argument for designing it against how the yacht is likely to be owned across its life rather than how it is bought.

## Jurisdiction is an outcome, not a starting point

Owners tend to begin with the jurisdiction. Roberts argues it should be one of the last decisions rather than the first. There is no best jurisdiction in the abstract; there is only the jurisdiction suited to a particular owner's use, operating area, residency and tax position, financing, and succession plans. Only once those are understood does the question of where to establish the structure become answerable.

Quadrant's own four decades are built substantially on Isle of Man work, which makes the firm's read of the jurisdiction worth stating plainly. By Roberts' estimate an Isle of Man structure is the right answer for roughly a quarter of purchases. The island is one of the leading jurisdictions for superyacht ownership, with a mature professional services sector, a stable legal and regulatory framework, and decades of experience advising owners, family offices, and their advisers. That makes it an excellent choice for many structures and the wrong one for many others. An owner buying a commercially operated yacht, a family with international tax considerations, or an owner with existing corporate structures elsewhere will each reach a different conclusion. Malta is often the more appropriate home; the British Virgin Islands and the Cayman Islands are established alternatives, each with its own advantages depending on the circumstances.

The jurisdiction is also only part of the picture. The quality of the ongoing administration and advice tends to matter more to the outcome than the name on the certificate. A well-managed structure in the right jurisdiction is almost always preferable to a poorly managed one in what looks like the perfect location. The question a first-time owner should ask is not which jurisdiction is best, but which is best for their circumstances.

## VAT and importation are decided with the structure, not after it

VAT and importation are easy to treat as technical matters to be handled once the purchase completes. Quadrant's position is that they have to be developed alongside the ownership structure from the outset, because the way the yacht will be owned, used, and cruised drives the import route and the VAT status directly. Establishing the structure first and addressing VAT later is the common error, and by the time it is addressed, decisions already taken have narrowed the options. Restructuring after completion is slow and expensive; revisiting the VAT or importation position retrospectively is worse, with the exposure measured as liabilities and penalties calculated on the value of the yacht.

Most owners do not need a complex answer. The early conversations tend to settle into one of a few broad models. A non-EU resident intending purely private use may find that a non-EU structure operating under Temporary Admission relief fits best, allowing the yacht to cruise EU waters without VAT paid on the purchase, provided the conditions are met. An EU-resident owner who wants VAT-paid certainty for private ownership within Europe may be better served by a Maltese structure, which offers continuity for a yacht that will stay in the European market. Where the intention is a genuine commercial charter business, a different framework is required: commercial structures can allow recovery of VAT on qualifying costs, but they carry regulatory, technical, and operational obligations that should be understood before the route is taken. In every case the jurisdiction is the product of the planning, not the premise of it. The earlier the conversation happens, the more options remain; the later it happens, the fewer.

## Private use against commercial use

The question is not whether to register the yacht privately or commercially. It is how the owner genuinely intends to use it. Registration is an operating model rather than a label, and the choice reaches into the structure, the VAT position, the importation strategy, and the compliance burden. The most common misconception, on Quadrant's account, is that commercial registration is a way to offset running costs by generating income. Chartering is closer to operating a business than to making the yacht available for hire: it brings regulatory requirements, tax obligations, operational responsibilities, and continuous administration. It also means the owner pays for their own use of the yacht, including the VAT due on their time aboard.

Private ownership should not be assumed to be the simpler option without the same scrutiny. The right answer depends on how the yacht will actually be owned and run, not on which route looks cheaper or easier from the outside. The decision should follow what the owner intends the yacht to do, not what it could do. Plans do evolve, and a yacht bought for private use may later be chartered, or the reverse, which is an argument for building a degree of flexibility into the structure at the start rather than for defaulting to the more elaborate option.

## What the annual fee buys

Every structure carries an ongoing cost to maintain, and to a first-time owner it can read as overhead. Quadrant declines to attach a single figure to it, and reasonably so: the annual cost turns on the size of the yacht, the structure, the jurisdiction, and whether the yacht runs privately or commercially. The more useful question is what the fee covers. A corporate services provider maintains the ownership vehicle, meets the statutory and regulatory obligations, and coordinates the banks, flag administrations, insurers, lawyers, and tax advisers around the yacht. In practice it becomes the central point through which those relationships are managed across the life of the asset.

How the fee is charged is worth understanding before it is agreed. Some providers charge a fixed annual fee, others bill for time spent; neither is inherently right, and the owner should know which model they are on and whether it fits the complexity of their affairs. Beyond the administration, a well-run structure separates the owner's personal assets from the yacht, which matters if liabilities arise, and provides a degree of privacy consistent with the transparency and information-sharing obligations that now apply. The fee is better read as protection of the owner's legal and financial position than as a line of overhead.

## The independence test, applied to Quadrant

The six-question test earlier in this chapter applies to a corporate services provider as squarely as to any adviser, and Quadrant's answer to it is instructive. Roberts' position is that a provider should never be the only adviser in an ownership decision. The provider's interest and the owner's are aligned on the thing that matters, a structure that is robust, well-governed, and able to adapt, but no single adviser should supply every answer. Lawyers should advise on the law, tax specialists on tax, and the provider should work alongside them rather than in their place.

The signal to be wary of, on Quadrant's own account, is an adviser whose recommended solution arrives before the owner's objectives have been understood, or who discourages a second opinion. Where significant tax planning, family wealth or succession, or cross-border complexity is involved, an independent legal or tax opinion is good governance rather than a mark of distrust, and a reputable provider welcomes it. That a firm which sells ownership structures says so is the point of publishing its answer here.

## The decision hardest to reverse

The ownership decisions hardest to undo are rarely the deliberate ones. They are the assumptions made at the very start. The load-bearing choice is not the jurisdiction or the company; it is how the yacht is genuinely intended to be owned, used, and operated, because that decision sets the structure, the VAT position, the importation strategy, the regulatory status, and the administration that follow. Build those on the wrong assumption and correcting course later is both complex and costly.

The clearest example is an owner who establishes a commercial structure to charter, then decides to keep the yacht for private use within European waters. The switch from commercial to private can carry significant VAT consequences, in some cases a liability calculated on the value of the yacht itself, along with changes to the framework, the regulatory status, and the operational arrangements that are far harder to make after completion than before it. Structures should be given a degree of flexibility where possible, but the original decision has to rest on realistic long-term objectives rather than on what might happen. The earlier an owner defines how they intend to own, use, and operate the yacht, the more choices stay open. Left late, those choices become consequences.
