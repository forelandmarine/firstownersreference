## 1. The percentage-of-value model. An inconsistency inside the chapter, exposed by his answer.

Richard names no percentage anywhere in his answers. There is no number-against-number conflict, and nothing he says bears on the percentage-of-running-cost split in paragraph 10. What he rejects is a basis: a running cost model built on a percentage of the vessel's value. His words are "rarely agreed", not never.

The problem is ours and it predates his reply. Paragraphs 4 and 5 argue that percentage-of-value is obsolete and misleading, because the percentage climbs mechanically as the asset depreciates. Paragraph 7 then tells the reader to budget 12 to 15 percent, which from the section context means 12 to 15 percent of purchase price, the basis we have just discredited. Publishing his answer alongside it puts a contributor calling that class of model a broker creation a page away from our own version of it.

Current copy, paragraph 6 of the essay:

> Working ranges from independent practitioner sources (Fraser Yachts, Ocean Independence, Foreland Marine) cluster between 8 and 15 percent for the first decade. For yachts over 40 metres, or older than seven years, the figure is closer to 12 to 20 percent. Charter-active vessels run higher still.

Current copy, paragraph 7:

> Budget 12 to 15 percent for a typical 40m to 50m new build operated privately at moderate use, and revise upward for charter operation, larger size, or older age. Do not let any party paid contingent on a deal closing tell you otherwise.

Masters:

> We have rarely agreed with a maintenance and running cost model based on a percentage of the vessel's value. It is a fallacy. A way to sell. A broker creation. It is not operationally logical at all.

The section is titled "The folklore of the 10 percent rule" and then issues its own percentage of the same quantity. That is the fix worth making whether or not Richard signs off.

Proposed replacement for paragraph 7:

> Treat 12 to 15 percent as a sanity check on somebody else's number rather than as a budget. The figure that survives contact with an operating yacht is built from the bottom: crew complement, the standard of presentation the owner expects, weeks of use, build quality, the preventative maintenance schedule, consumable burn. Richard Masters of Master Yachts, who represents owners through new build, is blunter about it. A running cost model based on a percentage of the vessel's value is, in his words, a broker creation. The calculator on this site is a line-by-line build for that reason. Do not let any party paid contingent on a deal closing tell you otherwise.

Paragraph 6 needs a smaller fix regardless of whether he signs off. It describes Fraser Yachts and Ocean Independence as "independent practitioner sources". Both are brokerage houses with new build arms, and both appear on our own conflicted list in the chapter 05 research. Calling them independent in chapter 01 and conflicted in chapter 05 is an inconsistency a careful reader will find. Suggest "practitioner sources" without "independent", or attribute the ranges to the firms by category.

## 2. Crew. Our question premise was wrong, not the essay.

On re-reading, the essay does not claim there is hidden crew cost. Paragraph 9 says only that crew is 30 to 40 percent and rises with size, which he does not dispute. The premise that the owner does not see where the money goes was in our question, not in the published copy. No essay correction needed.

What we get instead is a gap worth filling. He says crew cost is "always minutely detailed on the initial budget" and that the real exposure is turnover until the boat settles. Crew turnover appears exactly once across all nine essays, in chapter 07, and is never costed. Suggest a sentence after paragraph 9 of chapter 01, held until he can give a range:

> The line that moves is not the salary bill, which is usually forecast accurately from the start. It is replacement. Turnover through the first season, while the programme finds its shape, is the crew cost that arrives unbudgeted.

Update, 4 September. He has given a range and the caveats to go with it: turnover can be as much as 50 percent of a crew of 5 to 50 in the first year, it depends on leadership and the owner's taste, and he states it is not a hard fact. It goes into the Q&A in his words with the caveats attached. In the essay it runs as his observation, attributed, not as a figure: the range is a single fleet's experience and the pricing inclusion rule holds. Proposed essay sentence to follow the one above:

> Richard Masters of Master Yachts, whose firm has managed more than 50 yachts, has seen first-year turnover reach half the crew, and says how much of it arrives depends on leadership and the owner's taste as much as anything on the budget.

## 3. Warranty travel. Absent from the publication entirely.

He names it as a year-one surprise that "no one considers at the time" and puts it at hundreds of thousands. Warranty appears throughout chapters 05 and 06, always as a contractual and management question, never as a travel and logistics cost line. Nobody else we have interviewed has raised it.

This is the most genuinely new material in his answers. It belongs in chapter 01 as a year-one cost, and again in chapter 05 or 06 where warranty management is discussed. It cannot run as a figure under the pricing inclusion rule until he anchors it to a size band and a build period, which is query one in the approval draft. If he will not, the observation still runs; the number does not.

Update, 4 September. He declines to anchor it to a size band, on the grounds that the exposure does not select by size or by owner: a Dutch build relocated to New Zealand, a German build operating in Spain, a Turkish build in a French yard. He regards this as the fundamental point of the question. The Q&A now carries the relocation examples in his words. On the number, "hundreds of thousands" stays in his answer as his characterisation from a fleet of more than 50 yachts, since it is opinion in a signed Q&A rather than a published figure. It does not run in the essay body. The essay gets the observation only: warranty travel is a year-one cost, it scales with the distance between build country and operating region, and it is routinely left out of pre-launch budgets. Jack to confirm he is content with the figure standing in the Q&A given the earlier draft told Richard it would come out unanchored.

## 4. Use intensity. He resolves an adjacency the essay leaves open.

Paragraph 12 gives 4 to 8 weeks from Ocean Independence data. Paragraph 13 gives 17 weeks average from The Superyacht Report OpEx Survey. Both are cited correctly and they sit next to each other unreconciled, which reads as a data problem.

Masters explains the split. First-time owners are younger, time-poor, and buying into a schedule they do not control. Owners at 12 weeks and above are experienced and already cost-aware. The two figures are measuring different populations, and his answer is the bridge. Suggest a linking sentence between paragraphs 12 and 13 on sign-off.

His secondary observation, that first-time owners are now "extremely savvy" and demand answers, cuts against the passive first-time buyer the chapter implies in places. Worth a look at chapter 09 as well.

## 5. Firm description. Resolved by Richard on 4 September.

Richard has set the scope himself. Master Yachts acted as operational manager for 20 years, for more than 50 yachts between 20 and 148 metres, and is also new build representative. He is explicit that he is not only an owner's representative, and that the opinion he gives rests on the operational experience: few owner's representatives in construction or refit could write it without it.

This reverses the 26 August direction that the firm is not an operational manager. The contributor's own description of his firm governs. All Foreland copy describes Master Yachts as operational manager and new build representative, in that order of record, and the Q&A introduction now says so. His question five answer stands as returned: new build representation first, operational management second, is his ordering of the firm's lines today and is not in conflict with the 20-year record.
