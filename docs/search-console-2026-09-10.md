# What Search Console says, and what it changes

First look at real performance data, 10 September 2026, covering the three months to 8 September. Both properties, web search only. This supersedes the assumptions in `search-strategy-2026-09.md` where the two disagree.

## The position, in numbers

Foreland Marine took 58,060 impressions and 377 clicks over the period, a click-through rate of 0.65 percent at an average position of 13.3. The publication took 39,057 impressions and 106 clicks, 0.27 percent at an average position of 9.4.

Impressions are compounding fast. Foreland ran 8,362 in June, 20,537 in July and 23,017 in August. The publication ran 7,020, then 11,095, then 17,487. Both roughly tripled in a quarter, from a standing start on one of them.

So the first correction to the strategy is straightforward: I had been treating a set of terms as unwon that the estate is already ranking for. Foreland has 878 impressions for "yacht refit project management" at an average position of 19.7, which is the query the page was rewritten for yesterday. The publication's chapter one has 14,756 impressions at position 7.3.

Visibility is not the problem. Conversion at the result listing is.

## The problem, stated plainly

At its best positions the estate converts at a small fraction of what those positions should produce.

Foreland, across positions four to ten, took 32,914 impressions and 203 clicks. That is 0.62 percent where the ordinary expectation is somewhere between two and six. The publication, across the same band, took 29,530 impressions and 74 clicks, which is 0.25 percent.

Two pages carry most of it. The publication's chapter one holds 14,756 impressions at position 7.3 and has produced six clicks, a rate of 0.04 percent. Foreland's 10 percent rule article holds 15,176 impressions at position 5.1 and has produced twenty, or 0.13 percent. Between them those two pages account for over half the estate's total impressions and under seven percent of its clicks.

The oddity that gives the game away is that Foreland's click-through rate is higher at position 21 and beyond, at 0.82 percent, than it is at positions four to ten, at 0.62 percent. Ranking better is producing fewer clicks per impression. That is not how search normally behaves.

## Two causes, and how to tell them apart

The first is a metadata problem that was live for the entire measurement window and was fixed the day before this export. Forty-seven of the publication's forty-eight titles exceeded display length, some to 106 characters, and twenty-five descriptions ran two to four times over. Foreland carried sixteen over-length titles and twenty over-length descriptions. Every one of those listings was truncating mid-phrase for three months. The publication, whose title problem was universal, converts at roughly half Foreland's rate, whose problem was partial. That fits.

The second is answer absorption, and one line of the data makes it very hard to argue with. The query "what is a typical seasonal yacht refit cost, and what factors..." holds an average position of 1.5 across 114 impressions and has produced no clicks at all. A truncated title does not take a first-place listing to zero. Something is answering the question in the result page.

The same shape recurs across every question-formed query in the set. "Charter broker vs central agent owner's representative" sits at 8.7 with no clicks across 158 impressions. Three variants of "superyacht annual operating cost 10 percent purchase price" sit between 4.7 and 6.2 with no clicks across 267 impressions. Those are the queries the publication was built to answer, they are ranking, and the click is going nowhere.

Both causes are real and they compound. The useful part is that yesterday's metadata work is a clean natural experiment: it went live on 9 September, so the thirty days to 9 October will separate them. If click-through moves materially, the titles were the binding constraint. If it does not, absorption is structural and no amount of on-page work will change it.

## What that means, if absorption is confirmed

It would mean the click is the wrong primary measure for the informational half of the estate, and that the citation is the product rather than a route to one. That is not a consolation. It is what the AEO strategy was built for, and the T+45 results already show it working: three of seven anchor queries cited, with the publication's framing reproduced close to verbatim in generated answers.

It also means the two halves of the estate need different scorecards. The informational pages should be measured on citation share and framing adoption. The commercial pages should be measured on clicks, and they already convert: Foreland's owner's representation page runs 2.0 percent from position 5.8, SeaTime Tracker 2.8 percent from 6.3, the homepage 3.8 percent, and the publication's calculator takes 13.6 percent on "superyacht cost calculator" from position 3.7. Transactional intent still clicks. Informational intent increasingly does not.

## Demand the estate is not serving

Three gaps show up with real impression volume behind them.

"Cost of yacht interior refit" draws 801 impressions on Foreland at position 8.1 and a further 498 on the publication at 19.1, and has produced no clicks on either. Neither site has a page about interior refit cost specifically. This is the clearest content gap in the data, and it is a subject where Foreland's own project archive holds material nobody else can publish.

The build-management family is largely unserved. "Yacht build manager" draws 542 impressions at position 61.3, "yacht build management" 460 at 48.4, "yacht project management" 419 at 59.3 and "yacht project managers" 331 at 74.2. Roughly 1,750 impressions at positions that amount to not ranking. Foreland uses "owner's representation" throughout and "build management" almost nowhere, which is a vocabulary mismatch rather than a content gap.

The Large Yacht Code cluster ranks well and converts at nothing. The exact-match query with "compliance" appended sits at position 5.8 across 306 impressions with no clicks, and the LY3 variant at 8.7 across 144. The article is at position 7.7 overall.

Separately, the publication is picking up long conversational questions about family offices and charter brokers, at positions between 34 and 81. It has no material on either subject. Those queries are the shape that answer engines produce, and they are finding the site anyway.

## Housekeeping

Google is indexing both hostnames for three Foreland URLs, splitting signal: the 10 percent rule article shows 15,176 impressions on www and 699 on the bare domain, with technical consultancy and yacht management showing the same pattern at smaller scale. Canonicals and redirects are correct, so this should decay, but a domain-level property in Search Console would consolidate the reporting.

Geography is worth noting without over-reading. The United States supplies the largest share of impressions on both properties and the worst conversion, 0.32 percent on Foreland and 0.10 percent on the publication. The Netherlands and Germany behave similarly at better average positions. Italy, Spain, France and Australia convert three to five times better. The pattern is consistent with heavier answer-engine deployment in some markets than others, but the sample is too small to carry weight on its own.

## Revised order of work

Nothing should be built for thirty days. The metadata fix needs a clean measurement window and every new page dilutes it.

On 9 October, re-export and compare. The question is narrow: did click-through at positions four to ten move. That single number decides whether the next quarter is spent on listing copy or on accepting absorption and rebuilding the scorecard around citation.

In the meantime the work that matters is off-site, which was already the binding constraint and is now the only one, and the extraction of one dataset from the Foreland project archive. Interior refit cost is the obvious candidate, because the demand is measured, the gap is real, and it is the one subject where a proprietary archive beats a better-funded competitor.

The build-management vocabulary mismatch is worth a small on-page fix at the same time as the October review rather than now: the terms belong in the existing owner's representation and refit copy, not on new pages.
