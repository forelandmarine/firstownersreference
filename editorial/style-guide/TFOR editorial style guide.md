# The First Owner's Reference, editorial style

A working guide for anyone writing into the publication. Drawn from a close reading of the first edition manuscript: the nine lead essays, the data spreads, the case studies, the checklists, the Erica Lay guest Q&A, and the colophon. Every example is taken verbatim from the published text and cited by chapter.

The point of this guide is operational, not aesthetic. The publication's differentiator is not cadence. It is credibility architecture: evidentiary seriousness, named sources, structural framing, and a refusal to oversell. Cadence supports that. It does not produce it.

A second-order risk worth naming at the top. A style guide of this kind can encourage writers to engineer the voice. That is worse, in practice, than the patterns the guide is designed to avoid. The best contributors to the publication will read this once, internalise the priorities in section 1, and then write as practitioners writing for practitioners. The cadence rules in sections 3 and 4 are for catching drift in editing, not for staging on the page.

---

## 1. What the publication is for, and what makes it credible

The editorial position determines the voice. Get the position wrong and the voice will not land regardless of how the sentences are shaped.

**The publication exists because trade press is structurally less able to publish what it publishes.** The publisher is funded by Foreland Marine's consultancy work and accepts no advertising, advertorial, sponsored content, or paid placement. Contributors are unpaid. This is stated in the colophon and on the press page, and the rest of the editorial position follows from it.

**The six-question independence test in chapter 09 applies to the publisher.** The publisher's answers are on the colophon. Anyone advising on a yacht acquisition can be tested with the same six questions; the publication's argument is that those questions should be applied to everyone, including the publisher.

**Structural framing, not character framing.** A broker's incentive structure is a fact about the industry. It is not a fact about that broker's morals. The publication documents the structure. Where firms are named, they are named because the published record names them.

**Evidentiary discipline.** Pricing in the publication is either backed by published market data (Quay Crew, IYBA, SuperYacht Times, MYBA, BOAT International, Knight Frank) or by aggregated practitioner data anonymised across at least five projects. Single-source anecdotal numbers do not appear. This rule is verifiable on every figure in the manuscript and is the publication's most important defence against the charge that it is asserting rather than evidencing.

**Concessions are part of the argument.** Each chapter at some point acknowledges what it is not arguing. *"None of this argues against ownership."* (Ch.01) *"This is not bad practice so much as the shape of the market."* (Ch.03) The concessions are not softening. They are the precision that makes the structural critique stick.

The reader is assumed to be either a newly liquidated UHNW principal or a next-generation inheritor, both entering an unfamiliar asset class with advisers around them who are conflicted by their position. The reader is intelligent, time-constrained, and uninterested in being flattered. The voice that works for that reader is calm, specific, and unsensational.

## 2. The three registers

The publication uses three closely related registers depending on where a passage sits.

### Lead-essay register

Third-person editorial. Declarative. Cited. This is the dominant voice and the one most of this guide is concerned with.

Example, chapter 03 opening: *"The first thing a new owner often learns, months after the first contract is signed, is that the structure of the market places most of the people around the acquisition on the other side of the table."*

Twenty-eight words, one sentence, embedded clauses. The voice walks the reader from premise to observation without stopping for breath.

### Guest-contribution register (Erica Lay, future Q&As)

First-person. Conversational. Looser sentence structure. Industry idioms used unselfconsciously. Comma splices are acceptable here in a way they are not in the lead essays.

Example, Erica Lay, chapter 07: *"Owners are no longer just hiring for tickets and sea time, they want leadership, commercial awareness, crew management, charter understanding, and increasingly: emotional intelligence."*

A named industry voice on the record can break editorial conventions; the contribution is published as given, lightly edited for typography. The same construction in editorial prose would read amateur.

### Editor's note and colophon register

Plainer, slightly more direct. First-person plural ("we") is allowed here and not in the essays.

Example, chapter 09 closing: *"The First Owner's Reference is written for the reader, not for the trade, therefore yours is the only audience whose response shapes the next edition. If a chapter has been useful, tell us. If a chapter has been wrong, tell us with more force."*

## 3. Sentence rhythm

The publication mixes sentence lengths deliberately. Most paragraphs contain a mid-length sentence carrying the argument, one or two short sentences for emphasis, and one longer sentence where the position is qualified or evidence is built. No paragraph is point-context-end repeated.

### What the distribution looks like

A typical lead-essay paragraph spans sentences of roughly 7 to 40 words, with most in the 15 to 30 range. Sentences below 10 words do emphasis work. Sentences above 40 words are rare and usually carry a list or a cited finding.

Sample, chapter 01, "What you actually buy":

| Sentence | Words |
|---|---|
| "The cost categories compound in an unintuitive way: crew, not fuel, is the dominant line." | 16 |
| "Crew accounts for 30 to 40 percent of annual operating cost and rises with size." | 15 |
| The following sentence on captain salaries with citation | 38 |
| "Maintenance and repair, 14 to 18 percent." | 7 |
| "Insurance, 10 to 14 percent." | 5 |
| "Berths and marina fees, 8 to 12 percent." | 8 |

Medium thesis sentence; medium amplification; long detail with citation; then four short fragments listing data. The short ones are doing emphasis, not filling.

### Constructions the publication uses

**Semicolon to chain related independent clauses inside one sentence.** Reserved for ideas that genuinely belong together. Where a semicolon could be replaced by a period without loss, the period wins.

*"Time from offer to delivery is typically 6 to 12 weeks; capital is concentrated at closing; the yacht is a known quantity to the extent the survey reveals it."* (Ch.05)

**Colon to expand or list.** Used freely. Reads as evidence introduction, not as rhetorical flourish.

*"Six decisions shape how the yacht runs and what it costs: the captain hire, the crew structure, the management company, the insurance arrangement, regulatory compliance, and whether to run as a commercial charter yacht."* (Ch.07)

**"It is not X. It is Y."** Used to define what the publication actually means against a likely misreading.

*"It is not, as one might first read it, an oversight. It is the structural reality of how superyachts are bought."* (Ch.09)

**Short paragraph or single-sentence paragraph.** Used sparingly, to land a hard point the surrounding paragraph has built toward.

*"It is also wrong for most readers of The First Owner's Reference."* (Ch.01)

*"The yacht is not an investment."* (Ch.01)

**Embedded modifying clauses.** The publication will often add an aside inside a sentence rather than starting a new one.

*"The standard depreciation curve, drawn from broker-aggregated data at Yatco, IYC, and Yacht Hunter, runs roughly as follows..."* (Ch.01)

The clause "drawn from broker-aggregated data..." is the citation, folded into the sentence. The next sentence is not required to do that work.

### What the rhythm is not

**Not point-context-end, every sentence.** If every sentence ends with a clean resolution, the prose reads as machine. The publication will sometimes leave a transition partly unresolved and let the next sentence pick it up.

**Not 50-word sentences strung together with semicolons.** Branching is allowed where the branches are doing real work. Long sentences for their own sake read as over-corrected.

**Not parallel architecture across a list of four.** "Part one is X. Part two is Y. Part three is Z. Part four is W" was flagged by external review as the most legible AI fingerprint in earlier drafts. When the publication has four things to say in sequence, each is introduced differently or sentence length is varied across the four.

## 4. Paragraph architecture

Paragraphs in the lead essays are typically 2 to 6 sentences. They open on the substantive point. They close on what follows from the sentences before, not on a restatement.

### Openings that work

*"The pay differential between a good captain and a competent average one is small; the operational differential is large."* (Ch.07)

The opening sentence is the paragraph's whole claim. The rest is evidence. There is no warming up.

### Closings that work

The publication's recurring closing move is a declarative sentence that lands the implication. It is not a recap.

*"That arrangement is the default of the superyacht industry."* (Ch.03, retrocession economics)

*"That is information rather than judgment."* (Ch.09, after the ten-question checklist)

*"The cheapest yard is rarely the best decision; the yard most aligned with the work, with track record on similar yachts and a project manager who has handled comparable scope, is."* (Ch.06)

### Closings to avoid

Thesis-restatement ("In other words..."; "The point is..."; "What this means is..."). These are the strongest AI fingerprints in editorial prose and the publication does not use them.

Equal-weight resolution on every paragraph. Some thoughts hang in the air for the next paragraph to pick up.

## 5. Vocabulary

These are preferences with reasoning, not absolute prohibitions. Apply them at editing.

### Words and phrases the publication uses

- **"On the published record"** for industry positions and quotes.
- **"Practitioner working ranges"** for aggregated industry numbers.
- **"Broker-aggregated data"** for data sourced from broker reports rather than independent research.
- **"Materially"** used precisely. *"Materially better."* *"Materially below."* Not as filler.
- **"Structurally"** used to describe inherent industry conditions, not character.
- **"The honest position is..."** used where the publication is naming what it actually thinks, after walking through the alternatives.
- **"By industry default"** names standard practice without endorsing it.
- **"On a per-deal basis"**, **"on a contingent basis"**, **"on a fixed-fee basis"** preferred to looser commercial framings.

### Words and phrases the publication avoids

- **"Actually"**, **"really"**, **"honestly"** used as emphasis. The publication relies on the strength of the underlying claim and these adverbs flatten it.
- **"In other words..."** as restatement. Almost always cuttable.
- **"The point is..."** Same.
- **"At the end of the day", "frankly"**. Both read as conversational filler.
- **Luxury-marketing register**: *"game-changer", "best-in-class", "unprecedented", "best-of-breed"*. Not present in the manuscript.
- **Intensifiers that do not change meaning**: *"very", "really", "quite", "rather"*. Used sparingly and only where they change the claim.
- **Patronising glosses**: *"(meaning, X)"* or *"(in plain English, Y)"*. The reader is trusted to follow industry terms. New terms are glossed once, plainly, in the same sentence.

**On "critical" and "key".** Both are tolerable where they carry specific weight. *"Critical"* in a technical sense (critical to the structural argument, critical to crew safety) is fine. As a generic adjective for emphasis it weakens the sentence. The test is whether the sentence loses meaning if the word is removed. If it does not, remove it.

### Industry terms used without explanation

SYBAss, YORP, YORR, MYBA, IYBA, FCE, LMAA, MLC, ISM, P&I, retrocession, dual agency, central agency, charter-week net, agreed value, builder's risk, comfort class, Red Ensign Group, Cayman Large Yacht Code: all used without parenthetical explanation on second appearance.

### Foreign and proper nouns

Yard, designer, and place names retain native spellings and accents. *Lürssen*, not *Lurssen*. *La Ciotat*. *Røkke*. *Vlissingen*. *Genoa* (English-language convention where one is established).

## 6. Punctuation

### Em-dashes

**Never.** Replaced by commas, semicolons, periods, or a rephrase. This is an absolute house rule.

### Quotation marks

Smart quotes throughout, including for nested apostrophes.

### Semicolons

Used to chain related independent clauses inside a single sentence. Used freely but never decoratively. Where a semicolon could be replaced by a period without loss, the period wins.

### Colons

Used to expand, to list, or to emphasise a final element.

### Parentheses

Used for citations, definitions, and the occasional aside. Comma-bounded clauses or semicolons are usually preferred to parentheses for an aside.

### Lists

Inline lists (comma-separated within a sentence) preferred in lead essays. Numbered or bulleted lists in checklists, contract points, and explicit enumerations (the ten contract points in chapter 05; the ten questions in chapter 09).

### Numbered enumerations in prose

Open each item on the substantive noun rather than on "First, ...". Example, chapter 05: *"1. Stage payment loading. Shipbuilding industry typical: 50 to 70 percent of contract value paid before delivery..."*

## 7. Numbers, sources, citations

### How numbers are presented

- Currencies in full units. *"EUR 200,000 to 350,000 in diesel."* Never "200-350k". Ranges use "to", not a hyphen.
- Percentages spelled out. *"30 to 40 percent."* Never "%".
- Years inline. *"In 2025 the global market for yachts above 24 metres turned over USD 8.5 billion."*
- Ranges and bands carry practitioner data. Single-point estimates are reserved for cited figures from one named source.

### How sources are folded in

**Inline parenthetical.** *"(Watson Farley & Williams)"* after the sentence carrying the claim.

**Source-led where the source's framing is the news.** *"BOAT International identifies the largest single driver as the 100 percent depreciation provision..."*

**Trailing attribution in a separate sentence.** Where the source carries more weight than a parenthetical would.

### The five-projects rule

The colophon's standard for proprietary practitioner data: aggregated across at least five projects, anonymised. Single-source anecdotal numbers do not appear. This is the load-bearing rule on every cost figure.

## 8. Argumentative posture

### Structural framing

The publication argues about systems, not about people. Example, chapter 03 on Camper & Nicholsons:

*"Camper & Nicholsons therefore has, on its capitalisation table, an Italian shipbuilder that builds yachts C&N then sells. Disclosure of the relationship is not consistently made when C&N markets a Fincantieri-built yacht; the conflict is structural and permanent."*

The conflict is named as structural and permanent. The framing is the system, not the firm's character.

### Concessions

The publication concedes what it is not arguing wherever the concession sharpens the critique.

*"None of this argues against ownership. It argues against ownership undertaken on the assumption that the cost is what the broker said it was."* (Ch.01)

*"This is not bad practice so much as the shape of the market."* (Ch.03)

These are not softening. They are the precision the critical part of the argument earns its weight from.

### Closing the chapter

Each chapter closes on a sentence or short paragraph that lands the position. The publication does not summarise itself.

*"A buyer who avoids these five will not have bought a perfect yacht; they will have bought one whose flaws they understand. That is the right standard for a first acquisition."* (Ch.04)

*"The single most important is to engage an adviser whose interests are aligned with the buyer's, before engaging anyone aligned with someone else's. That is the argument. The rest is detail."* (Ch.09)

## 9. Editorial discipline against drift

These are patterns the publication and external readers have flagged in earlier drafts. They are not "AI fingerprints" to be theatrically avoided. They are habits that betray a writer who has not done the work of finding the actual argument and is filling the page with cadence instead.

The reason to catch each of these in editing is the same: the publication's credibility comes from saying things, not from saying them in a particular shape. Sentences that perform the shape without carrying the substance are the ones to rewrite.

1. **Balanced sentence structures repeated within a paragraph.** "The role is not X. The role is Y." Used once it carries weight. Used three times it reads as machine. Vary at least one of the constructions.
2. **Repeated rhythmic cadence.** Three sentences of similar length and shape signal that the writer is not paying attention to what each one is doing. Each sentence should carry a different beat.
3. **Over-complete framing.** Every position acknowledged, every counter pre-empted, every paragraph resolved. The result reads as defensive. Allow some unresolved threads. Trust the reader.
4. **Compressed institutional summaries.** Repeating SYBAss/IAMI/GUEST/YORP/YORR boilerplate in multiple chapters wastes the reader's attention. Use the long form once per essay; refer to "the register" or "the programme" after.
5. **Restatement constructions.** "In other words". "The point is". "What this means is". Almost always cuttable.
6. **Identical paragraph openings across a chapter.** Vary opening sentence structure. Some paragraphs open on a noun phrase; some on a verb; some on a citation.
7. **Perfect closings on every paragraph.** Allow some paragraphs to end on a hanging observation that the next paragraph picks up.
8. **Parallel "Part one / Part two / Part three / Part four" enumeration in prose.** When the structure is the point (checklists, contract points), the list form is correct. When prose is carrying the structure, vary the introduction to each item.
9. **Em-dashes.** None.
10. **Heightened verbs in observational prose.** *"Lands", "surfaces", "crystallises", "stands as", "speaks to"*. Replaced with plainer verbs (*is, shows, appears, is described as*) where the heightened verb does not earn its place.

A note on the spirit of this list. The strongest writing on the site is not "human-looking because it has been roughed up". It is persuasive because the writer sounds like a practitioner with nothing to prove. Catching the patterns above is editing work. Engineering the patterns into a draft is a different thing and produces visibly mannered prose. The right instinct is to find the actual argument and let the sentence shape follow from it.

## 10. Conventions for guest contributors

### Setup

A guest contribution is introduced with a short editorial note naming the contributor, their firm, their role, and the editorial relationship. Erica Lay's pattern is the template.

*"EL Crew Co operates from Mallorca and places senior and junior crew across the over-24 metre fleet. We put five questions to Erica Lay on the senior captain market, rotation, junior crew dynamics, retention, and the first-time owner's captain hire. Her answers are published as given, lightly edited for typography."*

The phrase **"published as given, lightly edited for typography"** is the publication's editorial commitment to the contributor.

### Attribution

Contributors are introduced by full name and one-line credential on first appearance in a chapter. Subsequent appearances use surname only.

*"Hannaford Turner's Justin Turner notes engagement typically begins at request-for-proposal stage..."*

*"Erica Lay of EL Crew Co reports that captains in the 50 to 70 metre range..."*

### Pull quotes

A pull quote is the line a reader would underline. It is not a recap, not a softer reframing, and not a counterweight to the chapter's argument. It is the load-bearing sentence.

### Voluntary contribution

No contributor is paid by the publication. The colophon says so. Any outreach to potential contributors says so, in writing. No copy in the publication should imply otherwise.

## 11. House conventions

### Capitalisation

Sentence case for titles, sentence case for section headings. Proper nouns capitalised. Abbreviations uppercase (YORP, YORR, SYBAss, IYBA, MYBA, MLC, ISM, P&I, FCE).

### Contractions

Avoided in the lead essays. Allowed in the guest-contribution register where they are natural to the speaker.

### Numbers as words versus digits

Plain numbers below ten as words. Numbers ten and above as digits, except where a number opens a sentence. Percentages always digits. Currency amounts always digits.

### Dates

Day-month-year prose: *"On 20 April 2026..."*. Years alone: *"In 2025..."*. Quarter-year shorthand: *"Q4 2028", "H1 2024"*.

### Currency

EUR, USD, GBP — three-letter ISO codes used inline. Never € or $ symbols in body text.

### Hyphenation

Compound modifiers hyphenated before the noun. *"first-time buyer", "five-year survey", "30-page contract"*. Open when used as a noun phrase after the verb. Practitioner ranges hyphenated when modifying a noun, open when standalone.

### "The publisher" versus "we"

Lead essays use "the publisher" or "the publication". The colophon and the closing editor's note in chapter 09 use "we". The distinction is editorial.

## 12. A self-check at editing

Run a draft through these questions before treating it as ready. The questions are diagnostic, not stylistic.

1. Are the substantive claims sourced? Numbers, particularly. If a number is in the paragraph and a source is not in the same paragraph or the next, fix it.
2. Is the critique structural rather than character-based? If the paragraph is moralising about a firm or a person, reframe.
3. Has the chapter conceded what it is not arguing, where the concession would sharpen the critique? If not, add it.
4. Any em-dashes? Remove.
5. Any "actually", "really", "honestly" used for emphasis? Remove.
6. Any "the point is", "in other words", "what this means is"? Remove.
7. Any paragraph where every sentence is the same length and shape? Vary.
8. Any list of three or four where each item begins the same way and prose is doing the structure? Vary at least one.
9. Any closing sentence that recaps rather than lands a consequence? Cut or rewrite.
10. Read the paragraph aloud. If it reads as four full stops in a row, break it up. If it reads as one long sentence in need of a breath, split it.
